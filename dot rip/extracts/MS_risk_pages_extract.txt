RESILIENCE IMPROVEMENT PLAN 31 The BRIC index assesses community resilience to natural hazards by using a framework based on these six broad categories and data variables obtained primarily from federal sources, which are converted into rates, percentages, or averages. All data is checked for missing values, except for a few cases where national averages were used to fill gaps. Data from smaller areas is combined to represent larger counties. Each factor is then scaled from 0 to 1, where a higher number means greater resilience. This scaling helps to standardize the data across all factors. Methodology: Because the BRIC is measured on a countywide scale, scores were assigned to census tracts based on the county the census tract is located in. To calculate scores: 1. Each of the six major category BRIC values were treated as separate datasets, where a county’s BRIC value was considered a single data point. 2. A dataset range was then calculated for each category, and the data was divided into an upper and lower half based on the range. 3. The upper half (more resilient counties) received a score of 0, and the lower half (less resilient counties) received a score of 1. Since the BRIC is an indicator of resilience with a higher value indicating higher resilience, the inverse of this was used to score Community Vulnerability. Census tracts with higher resilience scored lower because a more resilient community is less vulnerable. In other words: 0=more resilient (less vulnerable); 1=less resilient (more vulnerable). 4. The total Community Vulnerability Score for a given county was then obtained by summing the scores from each of the 6 individual categories. RESILIENCE IMPROVEMENT PLAN 37 Droughts Definition: The Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) defines a drought as a prolonged period of no precipitation lasting for a season or longer. Droughts are fundamentally caused by a significant absence of water, which can have widespread and severe impacts on various sectors. The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) categorizes droughts into four distinct types: • Meteorological Drought: Characterized by extended periods of dry weather patterns, leading to a significant reduction in precipitation levels. • Hydrological Drought: Typically a consequence of meteorological drought, this type is marked by low water supplies in natural water bodies such as streams, reservoirs, and lakes. • Agricultural Drought: Occurs when dry conditions adversely affect farmland, leading to reduced soil moisture and crop failures. • Socioeconomic Drought: Arises when the supply and demand for water resources are disrupted due to drought conditions, impacting economic activities and societal well-being. Data Source: To estimate the severity and potential impacts of droughts, FEMA’s National Risk Index is utilized. The Risk Index measures vulnerability to a risk relative to the United States on the scale of: • 5 points – Very High • 4 points – Relatively High • 3 points – Relatively Moderate • 2 points – Relatively Low • 1 point – Very Low Risk • 0 points – No Concerns or No Data is Available Methodology: Points were assigned to census tracts based on the Risk Index (Table 10: Drought Vulnerability).
